## 1. The decisions

Made by the user on 2026-10-11.

| item | chosen | against |
|---|---|---|
| O60 | **A grant withholds the whole work.** The six routes answer a work outside the grant as they answer a slug no work holds. Stored positions and notes are kept | leaving the routes as they are and closing the item as accepted |
| O57 | **A fixed cap of four concurrent verifies, set without a NAS measurement**, with the lockout checked again under the permit | one permit per CPU core; measuring on the NAS first |
| O138 | **Hide the rail's scrollbar and let the wheel scroll the strip** | a taller strip that keeps the bar; leaving it and correcting the spec's sentence |

**O57's cap was offered as "at most 76 MiB", and that figure is the
verifies alone.** The four hash routes are outside the cap and already run
one at a time (§0), so the bound on argon2's buffers is five of them, about
95 MiB at the row's 19 MiB each.

**Why no measurement.** What the cap bounds is memory, at 19 MiB a permit,
and that does not depend on how fast the NAS is. A slower NAS lengthens the
queue §2.2 describes for a client that presents a password on every request,
and the answer to that is an API key, not more permits. So no verify time
the NAS could report moves the number, and taking one needs the source and a
Rust toolchain there. The *Later* entry "Measure argon2's cost on the NAS"
stays, narrowed to the question it still answers: whether the parameters
hold.

## 2. The changes

### 2.1 O60: a hidden work is an unknown slug

**The rule: on these six routes, every request against a work outside the
caller's grant gets the answer the same request gets against a slug no work
holds.** Status and body both.

- Each of the six handlers asks `visible_editions_or_refuse` where it looks
  the work up today. A malformed body is therefore answered as it is now, in
  the same order, for a hidden work and an unknown slug alike.
- `get_truth` and `put_truth` gain the `Extensions` parameter the others
  have.
- An account with no grant limits pays nothing: `on_the_grant` answers it
  without the library.

**Inside a work the caller may see part of** (a work migration 15 divided,
O191's shape):

- `put_progress` and `put_notes` validate against the caller's filtered
  sidecar. An edition outside the grant is then refused in the words an
  edition the work does not have is refused in.
- `get_progress` leaves out a position on an edition outside the grant, and
  answers 204 when none is left. For an account with grant limits that also
  leaves out a position on an edition key the work no longer has. An account
  with none is still sent it, as today.
- `get_notes` sends the reader's own document whole. A note names only an
  edition its writer once saw.
- Truth is gated at the work and sent whole. §3 says what that leaves.

**The grant table says so.** `routes::grant_postures` declares what each
guarded route does about the grant, and
`auth::tests::every_route_declares_its_grant_posture` drives what it
declares. Progress becomes `RefusesAndFilters`, and notes and truth
`Refuses`. `GrantPosture::KnownGap`, whose only entry was truth, is deleted.

**Nothing stored is deleted.** A position or a note on a work the account
can no longer see stays in the store and is served again if the grant comes
back.

### 2.2 O57: four verifies at a time, and the lockout read again under the permit

- Four permits for each server, `MAX_CONCURRENT_VERIFIES`. Each of the five
  verifies runs holding one. It is `cover.rs`'s semaphore type, moved to a
  module both can use, not a second one written beside it. The permits are a
  field of the server's `AuthConfig` and not a `static`, so one process has
  one set and each test's server has its own.
- **On each of the three paths the lockout is read twice: before the
  request waits for a permit, as today, and again once it holds one. The
  permit is then held until the failure is recorded.** The first read means
  a caller already locked out costs neither a wait nor a verify. The second
  is what bounds a burst, since requests that arrive together all pass the
  first: at most four requests are ever between it and the record.
- **The bounds, for wrong passwords.** On the login route and on Basic, a
  burst from one address costs at most 53 verifies (the ceiling of 50 and
  the three others already past the second read), and for one address and
  name at most 13. The password change reads only its own key, for one
  address and account, so its bound is 13, behind the guard. A verify that
  passes records nothing: correct passwords are bounded by the four permits
  alone.
- **The order is permit, then the library's connection, and never the
  reverse.** Nothing that holds the connection waits for a permit, which is
  why the four hash routes take none. argon2 still runs with the connection
  released, as `from_basic`'s doc requires.
- A request waiting for a permit holds a blocking-pool thread and no buffer.
- A session cookie or an API key never reaches the semaphore.

**The cost to a reader.** An OPDS client that presents a *password* on every
request queues behind the cap: thirty covers asked at once are thirty
verifies, four at a time. At the workstation's 10.93 ms that is about 77 ms
of hashing before the last one starts. A client holding an API key pays
nothing: `from_basic`'s doc says the key path never reaches argon2.

### 2.3 O138: no scrollbar on the rail, and a wheel that moves it

- The rail takes a class, and `reader.css` gives it `scrollbar-width: none`
  and a `::-webkit-scrollbar { display: none }` rule for a WebKit without
  the standard property. Tiles are then the strip's full height in every
  browser that honours either.
- **The wheel scrolls the strip**, because without the bar a mouse has no
  other way to move it. A `{ passive: false }` listener on the rail itself,
  for the reason `App.tsx`'s wheel effect gives:
  - a vertical notch with no `ctrlKey` moves the rail **toward later pages
    when the wheel turns down**, which is leftwards in a right-to-left
    volume;
  - a delta that is sideways as much as it is vertical, or more, and any
    wheel with Shift held, is left to the browser;
  - `ctrlKey` is left alone;
  - a notch is sized by `viewport.ts`'s rule for `deltaMode`, which gains an
    exported count for the purpose, so Firefox's lines and Chromium's pixels
    move the strip alike;
  - a wheel the rail handles is never passed on, at either end of the rail
    included, so the page behind the bar does not scroll in scroll mode.
- `--strip-unit`'s two constants are measured again on the running app with
  the bar gone, and set to what is read.
- The comments in `reader.css`, `tiles.ts` and `Filmstrip.tsx` that explain
  the 15 px, and the three in `tiles.test.ts` and `Filmstrip.test.tsx` that
  point at them, are **deleted, not corrected**. The rows of O138 and O145
  and their notes keep the history.

## 3. Not in this PR

- **O58** stays open: an API key can still rewrite truth and alignments. It
  asks what kind of credential may edit shared documents, which is a
  different question from which works an account may see.
- **O191** stays open and gains a sentence: inside a work migration 15
  divided, truth entries for the edition outside the grant are still sent,
  and so are a reader's own notes on it.
- **How many requests may wait for a permit is not bounded.** A caller
  already locked out is refused before it waits (§2.2). What can still queue
  is a burst that arrives before its own failures land, and a flood from
  many addresses, which can fill the blocking pool with waiters. Each
  address is held to its 50 and the buffers to the cap, but requests behind
  the pool wait. A bound is another number nobody has measured, so this
  opens a row instead of guessing one.
- **The hash routes take no permit** (§2.2).
- **Firefox is not measured** for O138. O145 never got a Firefox reading
  either.
- No change under `web/` is expected for O60. The sidecar route already
  answers 404 for a hidden work, and the plan checks how the reader words a
  404 from each of the six.

## 7. Corrected while it was built, 2026-10-11 and 2026-10-12

**§1 and §2.2 said the cap bounds memory, and a measurement on the running
server showed it bounding less than that.** Four permits do hold the argon2
buffers *in use* to four. With glibc's default allocator they do not hold
what the server keeps: a burst of forty wrong passwords left a release build
of the branch holding about 600 MiB, unchanged thirty seconds later, where
`main` peaked at 627 MiB and at 709 MiB in the two runs kept, and in the one
that read it again was back at 33 MiB a second later. `main` allocates its
buffers together. Under the cap the verifies run four at a time on as many
threads as the burst is wide, and glibc keeps freed buffers in those
threads' arenas. The readings and the two
allocator settings that separate "in use" from "kept" are in
`docs/notes.md`.

So §2.2 gains one more rule, and the ruling of four is unchanged:

- **A verify runs in working memory its permit owns, and a server makes at
  most four of them.** The memory is allocated once and used again, so what
  a server holds for verifies does not depend on how wide a burst was or on
  the allocator.
- **Hashing a password runs in a working memory the process keeps**, for
  the same reason: the hash routes run one at a time, but on whichever
  thread serves the request. The process keeps as many as have hashed at
  once, so a hash does not wait for another's. In the server that is one,
  because each route that hashes holds the library's connection while it
  does.
- **A working memory is wiped after every run**, so what a verify or a hash
  left in it does not stay for the life of the process.
- **`argon2`'s own verifier, which allocates, is called only from
  `credential`'s tests**, where it is what the project's verifier is held
  to. `credential` no longer exports a function over it, and `argon2` is a
  dependency of `taiyaku-library` alone. While that function was exported, a
  verify written against it would have answered the same and run outside
  the bound.
- The bound of §1, five working memories, is then a property of the code.

§1's "Why no measurement" stands for the question it answered, how fast the
NAS verifies. What it did not ask was what the server holds after a burst,
and that needed measuring.

**And one thing does depend on the host, which §1 said nothing did.** A
request waiting for a permit parks a blocking-pool thread, so the rate of
password requests at which other readers start to queue is four over one
verify's time, whatever the core count. The whole-branch review measured it:
pinned to eight cores the branch was no worse than `main` at any rate it
tried and far better past about 350 a second, and pinned to sixteen, at the
one rate tried there, `main` mostly coped and the branch stalled. That is
the row
§3 opens, and the review's answer to it needs no bound: wait for the permit
in the async handler and not on a pool thread.

**§2.3 gave a wrong reason for cancelling a wheel at the rail's end, and §4
listed a hand check that cannot fail.** "So the page behind the bar does not
scroll in scroll mode" names a hazard the page does not have: the bottom bar
is beside the page area, not inside it, and the whole-branch review found,
in a replica of the reader's boxes, a wheel over the rail leaving the page
still with no listener at all. A wheel
the rail takes is still cancelled at either end, because the rail took it.
Nothing about the page behind the bar was fixed, and the hand check is
dropped.

**§2.3's "Firefox's lines and Chromium's pixels move the strip alike" is
true of the function and not established of Firefox.** `stripWheel` reads a
line-mode wheel and a pixel-mode one as the same notch. What Firefox hands a
page that reads a wheel's deltas before its `deltaMode` was not measured
here, and by the review's account of Firefox's source, which it had from a
summary, Firefox hands such a page pixels. A notch in Firefox may then move
the strip less far than one in
Chromium. It is left for a hand on a wheel.

**The ruling's cost was understated in §1.** Hiding the bar takes away its
thumb, which was a mouse's fast way across a long volume. A notch of the
wheel moves the strip about one tile. And §2.3's "without the bar a mouse
has no other way to move it" overlooked a sideways wheel, which the browser
still scrolls the rail with.
